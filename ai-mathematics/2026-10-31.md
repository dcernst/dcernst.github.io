<h3><a href="https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/25/disadvantages-of-designating-mathematicians-for-ai-output-releases/">Disadvantages of designating mathematicians for AI output releases</a></h3>
<p class="ai-mathematics__meta">Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, guest post on Terence Tao’s blog · 25 October 2026</p>
<p>AGMAI argues against AI companies selecting mathematicians to lead the interpretation of released results. While acknowledging the value of early expert scrutiny, it identifies risks involving unequal access, community autonomy, selection bias, and borrowed reputation. It recommends transparent releases on independent repositories and support for workshops and working groups allocated through independent nonprofit institutions. Read alongside OpenAI’s announcement, this position clarifies a substantive disagreement about who should organize and govern the work of understanding AI output.</p>
</article>

<article class="ai-mathematics__item" id="ozdemir-ai-grading">
<span class="ai-mathematics__tag">Teaching and assessment</span>
<h3><a href="https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/21582440261502588">Can artificial intelligence (AI) grade like a teacher? Evaluating skill-based open-ended questions in mathematics</a></h3>
<p class="ai-mathematics__meta">Hasan Fehmi Özdemir · Sage Open · 28 October 2026</p>
<p>This open-access study compares three teachers and four AI applications grading ten open-ended mathematics questions answered by 196 seventh- and eighth-grade students in Türkiye. The tools differed substantially: ChatGPT and Copilot tended to award higher scores, and agreement across individual raters was only moderate. The distinction between ranking students similarly and awarding the same marks is particularly useful for assessment design. Results concern specific consumer tools, one sample, and a single rubric whose scoring discretion may itself contribute to disagreement; they do not establish reliable autonomous grading.</p>
</article>

<article class="ai-mathematics__item" id="ranade-public-goods">
<span class="ai-mathematics__tag">Student formation and teaching</span>
<h3><a href="https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/10/29/ai-mathematics-education-and-a-public-goods-problem/">AI, mathematics, education, and a public goods problem</a></h3>
<p class="ai-mathematics__meta">Gireeja Ranade, guest post on Terence Tao’s blog · 29 October 2026</p>
<p>Ranade argues that maintaining human mathematical understanding is a shared responsibility that cannot be reduced to producing answers efficiently. Drawing on conversations with students, she connects motivation and career prospects with the sustained effort needed to develop expertise. She calls for demanding, supported learning, recognition of exposition and understanding, and resources for teachers and individual tutoring. This is an informed argument about educational priorities, not a measured forecast of jobs or evidence that a particular intervention improves learning.</p>
</article>

<p class="ai-mathematics__meta">Steven Strogatz · The Atlantic · 25 October 2026 · Gift link</p>
<p>Strogatz explores the difference between obtaining mathematical results and the human experience of discovering and understanding them. Through accounts of colleagues and students at Cornell, he contrasts enthusiasm for AI-assisted research with concern about training opportunities, recognition, and the joy of discovery. The essay presents mathematics as a creative and communal activity while leaving open how that value can coexist with increasingly capable machines. It is a personal reflection on mathematical culture, not a study of learning or career outcomes.</p>
