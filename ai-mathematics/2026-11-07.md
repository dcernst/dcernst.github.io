<h3><a href="https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/01/mathematics-has-changed-lets-act-like-it/">Mathematics has changed. Let’s act like it</a></h3>
<p class="ai-mathematics__meta">Frank Calegari, guest post on Terence Tao’s blog · 1 November 2026</p>
<p>Calegari argues that AI should prompt changes in hiring, publishing, and graduate training. He proposes evaluating contributions through their ideas, exposition, and broader direction, with more direct conversations in hiring and greater breadth in doctoral education alongside technical depth. He urges senior mathematicians to develop new questions rather than race to produce results. These are proposals for changing professional incentives, not evidence that the proposed changes have improved research or career outcomes.</p>

<span class="ai-mathematics__tag">Mathematical purpose and professional identity</span>
<h3><a href="https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/11/02/egoless-mathematics-in-the-ai-era/">Egoless mathematics in the AI era</a></h3>
<p class="ai-mathematics__meta">Karthik C. S., Amit Sahai, and Jie Xue, guest post on Terence Tao’s blog · 2 November 2026</p>
<p>The authors ask whether mathematical work derives its worth from understanding or from individual achievement and comparative ability. From a Platonic perspective, they argue that a discovery’s value need not depend on human authorship or superiority to machines. Their discussion distinguishes producing correct deductions from choosing illuminating questions and understanding their significance. This is a philosophical contribution to the debate over mathematical purpose, rather than an empirical assessment of AI capabilities.</p>
</article>

<h3><a href="https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.2038958/full">Editorial: Bridging barriers: technology integration in mathematics education</a></h3>
<p class="ai-mathematics__meta">Musa Adekunle Ayanwale, Gladys Sunzuma, and Brantina Chirinda · Frontiers in Education · 6 November 2026</p>
<p>This editorial synthesizes a collection on technology in mathematics education, including generative AI and intelligent tutoring. It emphasizes teacher preparation, equitable access, mathematical reasoning, and scrutiny of automated feedback. Its discussion of AI-generated solutions and assessment highlights the need for mathematical knowledge and defensible human judgments when checking outputs. The authors distinguish observational associations from causal evidence and caution against equating tool usage with learning. This is an editorial synthesis of varied studies, not a new trial establishing the effectiveness of AI instruction.</p>
